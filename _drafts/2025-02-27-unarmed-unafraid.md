*Really not about guns, but about anxiety, dig.* ... Gerald Holtom's original peace symbol inverted (as he wanted it to be) to signify unilateral disarmament, a revolutionary way to live in a world that loves war. 



It can be said that peace is the opposite of fear. A more common understanding, however, is that peace is the opposite *of war*. When wars are ended, disarmament is possible; think, swords into plowshares. But wars in this world rarely just end, and fear of legitimate threats converts easily to anxiety, that ever-present, overactive, redundant radar system that reads *everything* as a threat. In this world, weapons are rarely melted down, but are locked away in the gun safe for easy access when the shit goes down and we need to protect *our own*. We all know people who are prepped and ready. We might even have a shotgun of our own over the china cabinet, so to speak. But while we may need to live *with* the sword, we don't have to live *by* the sword. 

This last year I've been working through some anxiety of my own, having discovered that my lifelong projection of calm had fooled even me. I came to see that I spent most days, especially my *days off*, stressed and ill-at-ease. With nothing to fear, and no real threats to confront, my mind nevertheless got cloudy with anxiety, and stuck in a kind of tunnel vision with nothing at the end of the tunnel. To work the problem, I have searched scripture, practiced body and breath-work, and filled quite a few journal pages as I explored my feelings and some family history. I found I also needed *reminders*, in order to resist my brain's default behavior of grounding all incoming and outgoing traffic due to fog. Like people have done for ever, I use objects to remind me of important things: rings on my finger; beads or knots on string; symbols  around my neck, like the silver cross I often wear. For this season, I went in search of a sacramental object that could keep me oriented to the peace I was pursuing. I (somewhat obviously) looked into the familiar hippie symbol from the 50s. I didn't particularly want to hang a random symbol around my neck without understanding more about its original meaning. So I read up, and I liked what I found. 

Designer Gerald Holtom was hired to create artwork for an anti-war rally in England, and the symbol he produced was soon appropriated by peace movements around the world. In light of the sign's newfound purpose, Holtom became dissatisfied with the original composition, which incorporated two downward oriented 'arms' split by a vertical line, the semaphore symbols for 'N' and 'D', initials standing for 'Nuclear Disarmament' (the mark's original meaning and purpose). Thinking the original with its downcast arms too evocative of despair (and possibly naïve, because who believes the entire planet will give up their nukes?), he came to prefer an inverted version, an upsweeping semaphore 'U' and vertical 'D' that would combine into a more hopeful image and a more compelling meaning: *Unilateral Disarmament*. The new image challenges the individual, when faced with unending agression in a world that loves war, to *not live life armed for battle*. Turning the other cheek might feel like weakness, but somebody has to be the first to lay down their weapons. For me this inverted 'peace' sign, and its message, resonated as a challenge to disarm my anxious radar system and live without fear. It is a symbol that puts the power of peace in my own hands.

It's not for nothing that Holtom equated this new upside-down peace-sign with the biblical Tree of Life whose leaves were for the healing of the nations, and with the cross of Christ, who himself would choose to ride to his death at the hands of those who preferred power to peace, and made that trip on a scrappy donkey instead of a war horse. He faced his accusers, hands open, holding no weapons to wage battle, only bearing sharp words and the will to follow his path. This unexpected, unconventional power move, considered absurd by those closest to him (who in a couple cases had their own blades hidden under their coats) would earn him the title, 'Prince of Peace'. 

If we can choose to confront unrighteous power without weapons, we assert the need for a different kind of transaction, one that takes place under the banner of peace. If we can do this, we make a clear statement about the world we prefer to live in. When we choose not to live in fear or anxiety, though the world be not entirely safe, we might just find real peace, and the clarity to face troubles with a sharper mind, and a gentler spirit.
